The picture belongs to the overlay

An overlay can be in any of nine places, so the player's own furniture may be in none of them. That is the whole rule, and it is not a preference — the bar is pinned to the bottom edge at inset: auto 0.75rem 0.75rem, which is exactly where the commonest overlay of all goes. The two collide on a stock camera, and this is shipped behaviour rather than a fault in the mockup.

What is allowed on the picture, and what is not

Nine anchors, nine places an overlay can be. There is no corner left over to put a control in.

Stays on the picture

The overlay items themselves, their selection outline, and the inspector editing one — because that is what you came to look at, and because the inspector opens on the far side from whatever it is editing rather than at a fixed corner.

Moves below it

Stream picker, codec chip, snapshot, fullscreen, and the placement readout the earlier round put in the top-left corner — which was the same mistake in a different corner, since a top-left overlay is the second most common one there is.

And on the Live page?

Unchanged. There the stage is the window and there is no page underneath to dock onto, so the bar stays overlaid and auto-hides. This rule is the Overlay leaf's, where the picture sits in a card with room beneath it — the two pages differ because their layouts genuinely do.

Brainstorm — several overlays at once

Today there is one overlay string and the page can assume it. With several, three questions appear that did not exist before: how you reach an item that is not the one you last touched, what happens when two of them overlap, and what it costs the camera. The three arrangements below answer the first differently; the panel at the bottom is the same for all of them, because it is a fact about the hardware rather than a choice.

An item switched off, or one dragged off the edge by a resolution change, becomes unreachable — there is no list to find it in. That is a trap rather than a tradeoff, and it would need an answer before this could ship.

The same in all three — what an overlay costs

A region per item, per stream size

The overlay is hardware: one region is created per distinct frame width, not per encoder, because the glyph size is derived from the width. So three items on a camera encoding two sizes is six regions — and a snapshot that inherits the main stream's size costs nothing extra.

Running out is silent today

A region the hardware refuses is not a clipped overlay — it is no overlay at all, with a line in the log and nothing on screen. Whatever arrangement wins has to show the count before the save, not explain it afterwards.

Two overlays that touch

Regions are drawn in handle order, so overlap has an answer whether or not the page offers one. Worth deciding now: either the list order is the draw order and can be dragged (B), or the page refuses to let two items overlap and says so.

Decided — A

Picked by the reporter of #340, on a better argument than the one I had offered: B forces a new concept — a name for every overlay — where A can label a chip with the overlay's own text. He also noted that C's objection (an open panel covering the picture, so an overlay underneath cannot be reached) mostly dissolves once the panel can be dragged out of the way, which it now can. C still loses on the harder version of that trap: an overlay switched off, or pushed off the edge by a change of resolution, has no chip and no rail to be found in.

Still open, and still the one that changes the data model rather than the layout: what two overlapping overlays do. Regions are drawn in handle order whether or not the page has an opinion, so the answer exists already — it is only a question of whether it is the one anybody would choose.

Brainstorm — a logo on the picture

Half of this already exists: the Rockchip path draws a baked-in OpenIPC mark as an overlay region — a 200 × 200 bitmap at four bits per pixel against a sixteen-entry palette, bilinearly rescaled for each stream. So the camera-side question is not can it, it is whose bitmap. That makes the interesting design work browser-side, and it is all one problem: a logo is not a picture the camera can show, it is a picture the camera has to be told how to approximate.

1 · What the camera actually draws, and why the preview has to say so

Your file

mark.png

512 × 512 · 8-bit RGBA
soft gradient · anti-aliased edge
drop shadow

What the camera will burn in

200 × 200 · 4bpp

16 colours, quantised
hard edge — no shadow
rescaled per stream

6 of 16 used

On first-generation HiSilicon the edge is one bit

Those parts draw into ARGB1555 — a pixel is either fully there or fully gone. Every later generation gets ARGB4444, which spends its coverage on sixteen levels of transparency and can hold a soft edge. So the same PNG is a clean logo on one camera and a ragged stencil on another, and the only honest thing the page can do is render that camera's approximation beside the file. Nothing about this is fixable in the browser; it is fixable only by saying it.

2 · How it gets onto the camera — three routes

A · Drop a file in the browser

The browser quantises; the camera stores a bitmap.

Drop a PNG or SVG

The only route where the person never touches a filesystem, and the browser is the one place that can show the approximation before it is committed. An SVG also rasterises to whatever the stream needs rather than once.

Needs an upload endpoint and somewhere on a flash-constrained camera to keep the result. Quantising in the browser means the algorithm lives in two places if the camera ever needs to do it too.

B · Pick what is already on the camera

A directory, listed — the trick isp.sensorConfig already uses.

/etc/logo/openipc.bin
/etc/logo/site-a.bin

No upload path, no new storage question, and a fleet gets its mark from the firmware image where it belongs — one build, every camera, nothing to set per device.

Useless to the person with one camera and a PNG, which is most of the people asking. Getting a file there at all means SSH.

C · Both, and B is the fallback Chosen

Drop a file if you have one; pick from the camera if you do not.

Drop a PNG or SVG

or

Choose from the camera…

The upload is the feature; the picker is what keeps working on a camera whose flash is full, and what a provisioning script targets. They share everything downstream of “here is a bitmap”.

Two affordances for one job, which is one more than the common case needs.

3 · Answered
1

The browser quantises. Decided, and it is the answer that keeps this page honest: the camera stores a finished bitmap, so the panel is showing the actual pixels rather than predicting them. Everywhere else on this page the picture is the camera's own output and nothing imitates it — a browser guessing at the camera's quantiser would have been the first exception, and now there is none.

2

Several logos, not one. A logo is a region exactly as a text overlay is, so nothing in the camera distinguishes them and there was never a reason for the page to. They share the overlay budget — handles 10 to 109, a hundred of them — and the item row counts logos and texts together because the hardware does.

3

No artificial size cap — but there is a real one, and a real cost. 200 × 200 was never a limit; it is just how big the built-in mark happens to be. The genuine ceiling is the stream: a region wider than the picture is refused by the hardware, and a refused region is no overlay at all rather than a clipped one. Below that, size is a memory question rather than a permission one — a region is two bytes a pixel, so a full-frame 1080p decoration is about 4.1 MB, once per distinct stream size. Measured on the lab hi3516ev200 there are 13 MB of media memory spare in total, so one such frame is a third of what is left and two would not fit.

·

So the page does not forbid a large bitmap. It shows what the choice costs, next to the region count, and lets the person spend it — the same move the item row already makes. The only hard stop is the one the hardware itself enforces, and it is worth stopping at because the failure on the other side of it is silent.
